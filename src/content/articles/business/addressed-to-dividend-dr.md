Dear future me,

Congratulations. Somebody put you in charge. I am sure you feel like you should be doing something about that, constantly. That is what I want to write about.

Do you remember standing through a BYU game in a Bachmeier jersey? You knew the jersey did not help him throw. You wore it anyway, because you cared about something you could not affect. That is where superstition comes from. Not stupidity. Just love with no steering wheel.

You will do the same thing as a CEO. The all-hands speech, the values on the wall, the reorg, the star hire, the hour spent watching a competitor — it will feel like leadership. Much of it is the jersey.

The one thing you actually control is where money, people, and your own hours go. That is the strategy. Not the deck. Not the speech. **Your strategy is what you fund.**

Remember this next part, because it is the one you will forget. Joseph Bower and Clark Gilbert studied how companies really allocate resources. By the time a decision reaches the CEO, the real choices have mostly been made further down — by people deciding what to propose, what to champion, which customer to keep happy. You will think you are deciding. Often you are just approving.

So there are two illusions. The first is thinking you control the market. You do not. The second is thinking that because you *said* resources should move, they moved. Announcing is not allocating.

Your real work is the boring machinery underneath: what gets proposed, what the criteria are, what you kill to free up room, who gets promoted, and where you spend your Tuesday. Shape that, and the strategy follows. Skip it, and you are wearing the jersey in a nicer chair.

Look at last quarter's spending, headcount, and calendar. Write down the strategy those imply. Compare it to the one you have been saying out loud. The first one is true.

What did you kill this quarter? If nothing, you did not allocate. You accumulated.

You can still give the speech. Just give it about something you have already funded.

Best,\
C-Money
